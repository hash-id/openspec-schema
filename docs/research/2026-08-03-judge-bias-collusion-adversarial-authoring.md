# Research: judge self-preference bias and multi-agent collusion risk in `hrt-adversarial-authoring`

**Date:** 2026-08-03
**Scope:** `skills/hrt-adversarial-authoring/SKILL.md`'s Destructive Critic and Structural Auditor both run as the same model family, in fresh isolated contexts, with no cross-review round (neither sees the other's findings before the orchestrator merges them). This doc verifies four citations from an earlier, less rigorous research pass and adds deeper sourcing on: whether fresh-context-plus-same-model is sufficient to avoid judge self-preference/collusion bias, whether the Critic's existing "assume the author is overconfident" instruction is an evidenced mitigation, and whether adding a cross-review round would help or introduce new collusion risk.
**Status:** research and verification complete. No changes made to the skill yet — findings and recommendations only, pending an adoption decision.
**Sources:** 13 total, all verified by fetching the arXiv abstract page directly (one exception noted in §2, source 6).

## 1. Citation verification

Four citations carried over from an earlier pass were re-checked against arXiv directly:

- **arXiv 2410.21819** — "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge," Koki Wataoka, Tsubasa Takahashi, Ryokan Ri. **Confirmed real, correctly characterized.** Shows GPT-4 assigns higher scores to lower-perplexity (more self-similar) text.
- **arXiv 2604.22891** — title is **"Quantifying and Mitigating Self-Preference Bias of LLM Judges,"** Jinming Yang, Zheng Hu, Chuxian Qiu, Zhenyu Deng, Xinshan Jiao, Tao Zhou. **Confirmed real**, but the earlier pass's "+90%" magnitude does not attach to this paper — it reports a 31.5% average bias *reduction* from its own proposed mitigation across 20 LLMs, not a raw bias magnitude of 90%. That 82%→30% figure belongs to separate ensemble-mitigation work (see source 6 below, not independently re-verified).
- **arXiv 2509.05396** — "Talk Isn't Always Cheap: Understanding Failure Modes in Multi-Agent Debate," Andrea Wynn, Harsh Satija, Gillian Hadfield (ICML Multiagent Systems Workshop 2025). **Confirmed real, correctly characterized.** Debate degrades accuracy via sycophancy/conformity, especially with capability-diverse agents.
- **arXiv 2510.07517** — "When Identity Skews Debate: Anonymization for Bias-Reduced Multi-Agent Reasoning," Hyeong Kyu Choi, Xiaojin Zhu, Sharon Li. **Confirmed real, correctly characterized.** Introduces an "Identity Bias Coefficient"; finds sycophancy occurs more often than self-bias; anonymization mitigates both.

**Action needed before citing further**: source 6 below (the 82%→30% GPT-4o ensemble figure) was found via WebSearch snippet only, not independently confirmed by fetching its arXiv abstract page — re-verify that specific figure/ID before using it in any external-facing document.

## 2. Sources

1. **arXiv 2410.21819** — "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge." See §1.
2. **arXiv 2604.22891** — "Quantifying and Mitigating Self-Preference Bias of LLM Judges." See §1.
3. **arXiv 2509.05396** — "Talk Isn't Always Cheap: Understanding Failure Modes in Multi-Agent Debate." See §1.
4. **arXiv 2510.07517** — "When Identity Skews Debate: Anonymization for Bias-Reduced Multi-Agent Reasoning." See §1.
5. **arXiv 2604.16790** — "Bias in the Loop: Auditing LLM-as-a-Judge for Software Engineering." Documents judges shifting scores based on stated authorship, verbosity, declared peer consensus, and emotional tone — relevant to whether the Auditor/Critic roles could be swayed by incidental framing in the draft they review.
6. **Ensemble-of-judges bias-reduction work** (exact arXiv ID for the specific "GPT-4o 82%→30%" figure not independently re-fetched — flag for further verification). General finding: a panel of models including smaller/weaker-self-preference models with majority vote cuts self-preference bias by more than half on average; recommends judging from a different model family than the candidate, with a 3-family ensemble for launch-critical decisions.
7. **arXiv 2604.02668** — "Too Polite to Disagree: Understanding Sycophancy Propagation in Multi-Agent Systems," Kasprova et al. Six open-source models tested; giving agents peer sycophancy-proneness rankings (transparency about who tends to agree too readily) improved accuracy +10.5% absolute — a different lever than role framing or model diversity.
8. **arXiv 2605.00914** — "The Cost of Consensus: Isolated Self-Correction Prevails Over Unguided Homogeneous Multi-Agent Debate," Bertalanič & Fortuna. 10 identical models debating underperformed isolated self-correction (uncritical majority adoption, rationale-driven destabilization of correct answers, voting discarding already-correct answers), at 2.1-3.4x token cost for equal-or-worse accuracy.
9. **arXiv 2604.18005** — "Diversity Collapse in Multi-Agent LLM Systems: Structural Coupling and Collective Failure," Chen et al. Premature convergence/suppressed diversity is a property of interaction structure, not just model sameness — implicating dense/authority-driven interaction patterns as much as shared model identity.
10. **arXiv 2601.19921** — "Demystifying Multi-Agent Debate: The Role of Confidence and Diversity," Zhu et al. Debate underperforms simple majority voting absent two specific mechanisms: diversity of initial viewpoints and calibrated confidence communication.
11. **arXiv 2603.12123** — "Cross-Context Review: Improving LLM Output Quality by Separating Production and Review Sessions," Tae-Eun Song. The single most directly applicable paper to this skill's exact setup: same model, fresh session, no shared history, reviewing its own artifact. Cross-Context Review beats same-session self-review (F1 28.6% vs. 24.6%, p=0.008, d=0.52); the gain is from context separation itself (repeating review in the same session did not help, p=0.11). Does not test model-family diversity as a comparison arm, and overall F1 (~29%) shows a large residual gap even with context isolation.
12. **arXiv 2606.05976** — "The Self-Correction Illusion: Role Relabeling Gates Explicit Error Flagging in Large Language Models." Relabeling an error's source (own prior turn vs. external) raises correction rates 23-93 points; the paper frames this as a template/label artifact rather than genuine improved error-detection, and the effect is domain-dependent — fragile, not a robust generalizable fix.
13. **arXiv 2412.06593** — "Anchoring Bias in Large Language Models: An Experimental Study," Lou & Sun. LLM judgments are disproportionately swayed by whichever information came first; common mitigations (chain-of-thought, "ignore the anchor," reflection) are not sufficient on their own — directly relevant to the risk of a cross-review round where Reviewer B sees Reviewer A's findings first.

## 3. Findings by question

**Q1 — Citation accuracy**: see §1. All four original citations are real and largely correctly characterized; only the "+90%" magnitude figure was misattributed and should not be reused without re-verifying source 6 directly.

**Q2 — Does fresh-context + same-model decorrelate enough, or is model diversity necessary?**
Mixed evidence, leaning toward "fresh context helps, but is a smaller effect than genuine model diversity, and does not remove the ceiling." Cross-Context Review (source 11) is a real, positive, measured result for exactly this skill's mechanism — but the improvement (24.6%→28.6% F1) is modest, and the paper never compared against a different model family, so it cannot rule out that model diversity produces larger gains. Ensemble-of-judges research (source 6, unverified exact figures; source 2's confirmed 31.5% reduction) shows model-family diversity producing larger, more clearly evidenced reductions than context isolation alone. "The Cost of Consensus" (source 8) shows identical-model multi-agent interaction can actively underperform isolation — same-model plurality is not a reliable substitute for genuine diversity. **Net**: this skill's existing author→reviewer fresh-context split already captures most of the *cheap* mitigation available; it is evidenced as insufficient *alone* for the collusion/conformity family of risks, which respond more to model-family decorrelation than to role/persona framing.

**Q3 — Is "assume the author is overconfident, do not validate" an evidenced mitigation?**
Partial mitigation with a real but bounded effect, and the evidence is indirect (drawn from adjacent skeptic/persona-prompting literature, not this exact instruction tested in isolation). Adversarial/skeptic framing does measurably shift model behavior in adjacent studies, and role-relabeling effects (source 12) generalize across task types — so explicit adversarial framing is not mere theater. But: (a) the strongest supporting evidence for persona-based effects comes from activation-steering studies, not confirmed for plain prompt-text instructions at comparable magnitude; (b) some judge biases are documented as not fully fixable by prompt engineering alone and need ensembling/human calibration on top (source 5's authorship/verbosity/consequence-framing findings). The skill currently gives this instruction only to the Critic, not the Auditor — no source found here suggests structural/format auditing is inherently less susceptible to leniency or sycophancy drift than content judging; if anything, role-relabeling generalizing across task types (source 12) suggests the Auditor would likely benefit from an analogous instruction too.

**Q4 — Would a cross-review round help or introduce new collusion risk?**
Evidence points toward net risk without added structure — avoid a naive "show Reviewer B what Reviewer A found" round. Anchoring-bias research (source 13) shows LLM judgments are disproportionately swayed by whatever comes first, and explicitly shows that simple mitigations like "reflect independently" are not sufficient on their own. "Too Polite to Disagree" (source 7) and "Talk Isn't Always Cheap" (source 3) both show LLM peer-exchange defaults toward sycophancy/conformity rather than genuine challenge unless something external structurally forces disagreement. The skill's current design — independent reviewers, merged without cross-exchange, explicitly "do not adjudicate between reviewers by LLM judgment" — is well-aligned with what "The Cost of Consensus" (source 8) recommends: isolated correction beats unguided homogeneous debate. If a cross-review round were added, it would need real structural teeth to avoid becoming a collusion vector (e.g., each reviewer scores the other's findings on a rubric before any exchange, per source 10's confidence-modulation approach; or the round only flags conflicts for the human, never letting either reviewer revise findings after seeing the other's). A plain "let them look at each other's output and reconcile" round is the pattern most consistently linked to accuracy degradation in this literature.

## 4. Recommendations

1. **Model/persona diversity note**: add, as a "where infrastructure allows" recommendation (not a requirement), that one of the two reviewers should ideally run on a different model family. The ensemble/cross-family-judge literature (sources 2, 6) shows meaningfully larger effect sizes than context isolation alone provides — but Cross-Context Review (source 11) confirms the existing same-model-fresh-context design is still a real, non-trivial mitigation on its own, so this is a strength-upgrade, not a fix for something broken.
2. **Strengthen the Auditor's framing**: give the Structural Auditor an adversarial instruction analogous to the Critic's ("assume traceability/structure gaps exist and have been overlooked — do not assume the draft is compliant until checked"). No source found here exempts structural checks from sycophancy/leniency drift, and role-relabeling effects generalize across task types.
3. **Do not add a naive cross-review round.** The debate-collusion and anchoring literature both point toward net degradation absent real structural constraints (independent scoring before exchange, or restricting any exchange to flagging conflicts for the human rather than allowing reconciliation). Adding an unstructured round would also cut against the skill's own existing principle of not adjudicating between reviewers by LLM judgment.
4. **Open gap, not resolved by this pass**: no source found here validates the specific wording "assume the author is overconfident" against alternative adversarial phrasings — if a tighter evidence base is wanted, that requires either an ablation of this repo's own or a narrower literature search on exact-phrasing effects. Flagged as future work, not resolved here.
